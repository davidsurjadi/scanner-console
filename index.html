What this is

Four tabs, in the order you read them. The first is the decision; the next two are the workings behind it; the last is this page. The engine runs the other way round — the screen finds candidates before anything can be recommended — but that is a fact about the system, not about how to read it. Nothing here places an order or sizes a position.

Read it left to right

  1. Today's Plan. Triggers are takeable at today's price; the watch list is takeable if price returns to a level. If both are empty the day is done — a valid answer, not a failure. The count above the tabs is the short version, so you can stop here on most days.
  2. Why it scored when you want the evidence behind a name, or what moved since yesterday.
  3. Is it a good business before committing to anything on the plan. Earnings inside your holding window is the single most common reason to skip an otherwise good setup.

Today's Plan, block by block

Each block answers a different question. Two are lists of live opportunities; three are history. Confusing the two kinds is the easiest mistake this page can cause, so the question each one answers is printed beside it.

Everything above answers "what should I do today". These two answer "is this page telling me the truth". Both are deliberately shown even when there is nothing to report, because an empty panel and a broken pipeline must never look the same.

Scan health gives one row per market: when it last scanned, how many tickers it covered, and how many came back unscored. A market missing from this list did not scan. Stale means the newest snapshot is more than 7 days old, so every number drawn from that market is describing an older market than the one trading now.

Ticker requests is the queue of symbols proposed for a watchlist. The panel is read-only, and stays that way for a plain reason: this console is regenerated from the archive every day, so anything typed into it would be overwritten by the next render. Tickers are added and approved on a private page, and the ID routine validates them and drops the snapshot this panel reads. Four outcomes, and they are not interchangeable. Pending is waiting on you. Approved means you said yes and no routine has run since. Promoted means the symbol returned real price data and is now being scanned. Failed means you said yes but no provider could return usable bars, so nothing was added — that one shows the reason, and it is the only outcome that needs you to do something. Not synced is a fifth state and not an empty queue: it means no routine has read the request page yet, so the queue is unknown rather than known to be empty.

What a currency has instead of accounts

A pair has no earnings, no return on equity and no balance sheet, so every column on 3 · Is it a good business is meaningless for one. That tab says so and points here. It does not follow that a currency has no fundamentals. It has different ones, and they all sit on 2 · Why it scored, under what moves these pairs.

What rates are priced to do. Higher policy rates draw money toward a currency, so what a central bank is expected to do is the nearest thing a pair has to a fundamental. Each pair is split into its two sides — NZDJPY is New Zealand dollars against yen — and each side shows what is priced and by how much those odds moved. What matters is the gap between the two, not either alone: two currencies both priced to hike are not diverging.

The plain reading beside them says which side the gap favours, such as rate path favours JPY over NZD and those odds are rising. Read that as a statement about the rate differential and nothing more. It is not a forecast of the price; what the pair actually does is what the score is for, and the two can disagree.

What is scheduled. The events due are named, with the forecast against the previous reading, because a count would not tell you whether the risk is payrolls or a revision to a PMI nobody trades. An event on either currency in the pair can move it.

How the big players are positioned. COT is the net position of leveraged funds, and retail is the crowd on the other side of them. COT lags on purpose: the CFTC publishes on a Friday for the previous Tuesday, so by Thursday the figure is nine days old. The panel prints the date it applies to rather than implying it is current.

Three of those columns are not ours. Their bias, COT and retail come from an outside research digest, named on the panel with the issue it came from. They feed nothing. The gate is uncalibrated for currencies as it is, and letting somebody else’s opinion into the score would change a number measured on shares only.

Which makes read the column worth your attention. It compares our signed score with their bias and says whether the two point the same way. Disagrees, in amber, is the interesting case: two independent reads of one pair reaching opposite conclusions. It is a prompt to look, not a signal to trade, which is why it is not coloured like one. No read means one side had no view, or used wording we do not recognise — their vocabulary drifts, and an unfamiliar label is reported as unreadable rather than guessed at.

Practitioner conventions, not laws — they come from trading-education sources rather than peer review, so treat them as the range experienced traders argue within. The exception is sample size, which is ordinary statistics and is not up for debate.

Is there a Buffett guide to follow?

There is a published one. Berkshire's Acquisition Criteria have run in the annual report for decades: demonstrated consistent earning power, good returns on equity with little or no debt, management in place, simple businesses, and a stated price. Two things to be clear about before borrowing them.

They are criteria for buying whole businesses, and several have no analogue for a share you can sell on Tuesday. And they argue against most of what the screen does: the framework has no opinion on a 20-day horizon, a stop-loss, or a support level. It is a case for owning a business through exactly the period this scanner would have closed the position and moved on. Using both means running two sleeves under different rules — not blending them into one score.

The fundamentals columns

None of these decide a trade. They are the veto stage: a setup that survives them is not endorsed, it merely has nothing obviously wrong with it. Every one is meaningful only against its sector — a 40× P/E is ordinary in software and alarming in a utility — so read the row, never the number.

Sector
What the comparison should be made against. Sector medians are built from this scanner's own watchlists and carry an as_of date; a sector with fewer than five usable constituents is deliberately absent rather than estimated.
P/E
Trailing, not forward. A negative or missing value usually means no earnings rather than a data failure, and the two are not the same thing.
ROE
Return on equity. High ROE on high debt is leverage rather than quality, so it is read next to D/E, never alone. This is the one column that maps cleanly onto Berkshire's published criteria.
Margin
Net margin. Useful for direction of travel more than level: what a good margin is varies more by industry than almost any other figure here.
D/E
Debt to equity, as a percentage. Balance-sheet structure differs enough by sector that a bank and a software company cannot be compared on it at all.
Short%
Short interest as a share of free float. US only. Its absence for SG and ID is normal and is not a fetch failure.
Earnings / surprises
How many recent quarters beat estimates, and by how much on average. "annual results only" means the feed carries results but no estimates to compare them against — an answer, not an outage.

When it says nothing to do

An empty trigger list is the most common single outcome and it is a result, not a malfunction. A structural entry lives at a level, and price spends most of its time away from levels — so on a typical day most qualifiers are waiting, not ready.

The failure mode this console is built against is not missing a trade. It is taking one at a price the plan never called for, because the screen looked empty and something had to be done.

What it will not tell you

Position size (that needs account balances), intraday prices (this renders from daily scans), or whether any single trade will work. The hit rate behind Avg R/trade is a population average across thousands of past observations, measured on a 20-day-forward proxy that the paper tracker is in the process of replacing with real target-before-stop outcomes.

A row here is a plan with conditions attached, not advice and not an instruction.
